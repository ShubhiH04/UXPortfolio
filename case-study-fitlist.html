FitList

Buying women's jeans is a guessing game that ends in self-doubt. FitList turns it into a shared, confidence-building experience.

The problem

Sizing has no standard, brands use vanity sizing, and fit models rarely reflect diverse bodies. Shoppers slide from optimism to giving up.

What I did

Led research and journey mapping, used behavioral psychology to set design goals, and led the denim identity quiz and lean canvas research.

The result

Testers responded best to the quiz, the friends page, and the Pinterest-style board. They were unlikely to retake the quiz, our main open problem.

Additional Thoughts

This project taught me to design from emotion first. Using Kahneman's experiencing and remembering selves, I mapped how shopping should feel at each step, then shaped the screens around it. The friends page, with no public posts or follower counts, came directly from wanting shoppers to feel supported, not judged. The question I'll carry into every project: does the story I designed for the user show up in the product?

Where it started: the first versions

The first versions, made in Figma Make and Claude, had an onboarding quiz, denim identity reveal, recommendations feed, virtual closet, and community page.

Then we made the community the focus.

The final design

A Pinterest-style board, a friends page, direct fit sharing, and a whole-look board.

A size 4 in one store is not a size 4 in the next.

Research: shopping for jeans breaks down at the first size

What we found

  • No standard to rely on. A size 4 in one store tells you nothing about the next, so past purchases are useless as a reference.
  • Vanity sizing. Brands label jeans with a smaller size to appeal to customers, which makes numbers even less trustworthy.
  • Fit models that don't reflect diverse bodies, so many shoppers are guessing from the start.
  • Returns add cost. Our persona, Tiffany, 29, loves trying new styles but hates traveling back to the mall to return jeans that didn't fit.
  • The emotional arc falls. Mapping her journey showed optimism at the rack turning into frustration and giving up.

Design decisions

1. Design for how shopping is remembered, not only how it feels. Using Kahneman's experiencing and remembering selves, we aimed for control in the moment and an "aha" ending with jeans that fit.

2. Replace guesswork with a "denim identity" quiz. Inspired by Spotify Wrapped and BuzzFeed quizzes, it gives personalized, cross-brand recommendations that also work in store.

3. Make the community personal, not public. Solo shopping is isolating, so we built a Pinterest-style board and a friends page with no public posts or follower counts, just honest feedback from people you know.

Where FitList fits in the market

True Fit and fitAnalytics live on partner retailer sites and are built for retailers. FitList is a standalone shopper app that works online and in store, with a community.

Key learning: design from emotion first, then shape the screens around it.

What testing showed

The quiz and denim identity, the Pinterest-style board, the friends page, and shopping the whole look all resonated.

5 user tests, 20 minutes each

What's still unsolved

Users are unlikely to retake the quiz, so recommendations could go stale as fit changes. Next, I'd update recommendations based on activity on community pages. Testers also asked for an outfit-of-the-day leaderboard.

Psychology framework
  • Experiencing self: more coherent, controlled effort; a predictable process; progress toward an attainable goal.
  • Remembering self: an "aha" moment, then satisfaction and confidence in the purchase.
  • Be-Goals (Hassenzahl): competence, with pragmatic, hedonic, and eudaimonic goals.
  • Meaning (Huta): restoring coherence so shoppers focus less on frustration and lack of control.
  • Relatedness: moving from an isolated struggle to seeing yourself in others.
